G2C: an escrow for digital goods

When strangers trade online, one side usually has to trust the other first: the buyer pays and hopes, or the seller delivers and hopes. G2C removes that step with one guarantee: the goods and the money move together, or neither moves. Its first use is trading in-game items, where players already pay real money on grey-market sites. I built the escrow and then simulated what it would take to run it every day.

How it works

A trade runs through its steps in a fixed order, from the easiest to undo to the hardest. The first step needs a payment that can be held: taken from the buyer, but not yet claimed, so it can still be returned. Card payments and bank transfers can't do that this way, and the Lightning Network can. When the buyer pays, the payment is locked at G2C's Lightning node as a hold invoice: G2C can claim it or give it back, and only G2C holds the secret that claims it. Then the game freezes the item at the seller. G2C pays the seller, claims the buyer's payment, and the game hands the item to the buyer. If anything fails before the seller is paid, the item is unfrozen and the lock released, so the buyer gets the money back without anyone acting.

The escrow is a small Python service. It advances each sale by one step at a time and saves every step before taking the next, so after a crash it carries on from the state it saved. Every step can be repeated safely. Each change is written to an append-only, hash-chained event log, and every sat to a double-entry ledger. A few rules close the ways to pay the seller and refund the buyer both: it never refunds while the seller's payment might still land, it only pays while the buyer's locked payment has enough time left, and after an error on claiming it asks the node what happened instead of guessing.

The escrow runs on Core Lightning in a local test network, with a demo game and drills that crash it and cut its connections, and on LND for mainnet. An earlier version of the contract has completed one real sale on mainnet.

The simulation

Before running it for real money, I wanted numbers on what running it takes: how much G2C pays out before it collects, which fee it needs, how often a buyer gets a refund and when an operator should be woken. There is no traffic yet to measure, so I ran the escrow's own state machine, unchanged, against a simulated Lightning node, game and buyers, each week on its own clock.

Every rate in the simulated world is an assumption with a range, and each week draws its own values from those ranges, so a conclusion only counts when it holds across them. Faults come in bad periods, outages have a long tail, and the hardest faults are lost answers, where the node pays the seller and the escrow never hears back. To check that the checks would catch a broken escrow, I broke it twice on purpose; they found 43 and 304 broken sales. The escrow as it is had none. Fifty weeks then ran under four fee structures, the same weeks under each.

What carries over

Most of the work applies to any system that moves money on someone else's behalf. Every step can be repeated after a crash without paying twice. The escrow never refunds while a payout might still land, and treats "timed out" as an unknown outcome. Every sat is booked in a double-entry ledger, and every finished sale is checked against what actually happened. The alert thresholds come from the same telemetry queries a live dashboard would run.

What I found

Each seller payout is fronted for a few seconds, so the most G2C pays out before it collects is about the week's largest sale. The node needs spare balance of that size, and a maximum sale amount would cap it. Profit follows the fee's percentage, refunds hardly depend on the fee, and below about 0.5% small sales start to find no route within their fee. Most refunds come from the game refusing to reserve an item; failed payouts matter much less. The write-up turns the spreads into alert thresholds for a dashboard, and says which numbers to measure first once G2C is live.
